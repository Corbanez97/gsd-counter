# Summary: Ground State Degeneracy, Constraints, and Symmetries in Commuting-Projector Models

## 1. The core relationship (starting point: arXiv:2406.19275)

In commuting-projector (stabilizer) Hamiltonians built from local operators \(O_p\), the ground state degeneracy (GSD) is controlled by redundancies among the \(O_p\). A set of exponents \(t_p \in \mathbb{Z}_2\) satisfying

$$\prod_p O_p^{t_p} = \mathbb{1}$$

identifies a *relation* — a way some \(O_p\)'s are not independent. If the space of such solutions has dimension \(2N_c\), then

$$\mathrm{GSD} = 2^{2N_c}.$$

This is the standard stabilizer-formalism statement "\(\mathrm{GSD}=2^{n-\mathrm{rank}(\text{stabilizer group})}\)," with the rank computed as (number of generators) minus (dimension of the relation space). In the fractal subsystem-symmetry paper, this relation space has a recursive, Sierpinski/Pascal's-triangle-mod-2 structure (Figure 4), encoded via a polynomial representation \(S_j(x) = \sum_i t_{j,i}x^i\).

**Key takeaway:** the number of constraints = the dimension of the kernel of the map generated by the \(O_p\), and GSD follows directly by rank–nullity.

## 2. Is this only true for commuting-projector Hamiltonians?

**Yes.** The argument relies on the \(O_p\) mutually commuting, so they can be simultaneously diagonalized and the ground space is exactly their joint +1 eigenspace — a pure linear-algebra (rank–nullity) statement. For non-commuting Hamiltonians there is no "check matrix" to take a kernel of; degeneracy must instead come from representation theory, TQFT data, tensor-network arguments, or numerics.

## 3. Reconstructing symmetries from membranes

This is possible and is a known, general procedure — not specific to one paper. In gauging dualities between subsystem-symmetric spin models and fracton/stabilizer codes:
- Products of \(O_p\) over a bulk region collapse to boundary "membrane" operators.
- These membranes are the image, under gauging, of the original (ungauged) model's symmetry generators.
- "Ungauging" — recovering the symmetry group from the stabilizer/membrane structure — is an established inverse procedure (Kubica–Yoshida, Vijay–Haah–Fu).

**Important subtlety identified later in the discussion:** a *fully closed* relation \(\prod O_p^{t_p}=1\) is, taken literally, the identity — not a symmetry operator. The membrane operator comes from *truncating* that same relation partway through the lattice; what's left over at the cut is the physical membrane/symmetry generator. So: the kernel vector's support pattern is the right object, but you must read it off as an **open/truncated** operator, not apply it as a full closed loop.

## 4. Does the Sierpinski-type rule in Figure 4 generalize to any dimension/operator choice?

- The **method** (representing operators as polynomials over \(\mathbb{Z}_2[x,y,z,\dots]\) and solving for the kernel) is fully general — it applies to any translation-invariant commuting-projector Hamiltonian in any dimension. This is Haah's polynomial/Laurent-polynomial formalism.
- The **specific recursive rule** (Sierpinski/Pascal's-triangle-mod-2) is *not* universal — it's a consequence of this particular operator's geometry. Different operator choices generically yield different recursion/cellular-automaton rules.

## 6. Does this only work at the exactly solvable point, or can it describe a whole phase?

**Yes, it extends beyond the fine-tuned point**, via rigorous stability results:

- **Bravyi, Hastings, Michalakis**, "Topological quantum order: stability under local perturbations," arXiv:1001.0344 — requires the *unperturbed* Hamiltonian to be a commuting-projector sum, but shows that under a weak local perturbation that keeps the gap open, the ground space splits by only an exponentially small amount — the GSD computed at the stabilizer point survives (approximately) even once the Hamiltonian is no longer commuting.
- **Hastings & Wen** (quasi-adiabatic continuation, 2005) — the technical tool producing a quasi-local unitary connecting the solvable point to the perturbed Hamiltonian.
- **De Roeck, Khemani, Li, O'Dea, Rakovszky**, "LDPC stabilizer codes as gapped quantum phases," arXiv:2411.02384 — explicitly frames stabilizer codes as "representative fixed points which capture the properties of an entire phase of matter."
- Caveat for fracton models: GSD is sub-extensive and number-theoretically sensitive to system size (UV/IR mixing), so stability arguments are more delicate than for conventional topological order; type-II codes (like Haah's) are harder to treat rigorously than foliated type-I models.
- For genuinely single-projector / frustration-free (non-stabilizer) Hamiltonians: **Michalakis & Zwolak**, "Stability of Frustration-Free Hamiltonians," Commun. Math. Phys. (2013) — the natural generalization of the BHM stability result.

**Using multiple commuting-projector points to map a whole phase diagram:** this is standard practice — interpolate between two (or more) exactly-solvable stabilizer points and track how GSD splits/changes to locate phase transitions. Canonical example: **Trebst, Werner, Troyer, Shtengel, Nayak**, "Breakdown of a topological phase," arXiv:cond-mat/0609048 (toric code ↔ trivial paramagnet, transition diagnosed via degeneracy splitting).

## 7. Your proposed numerical generalization to 3D (and its subtleties)

**Your idea:** instead of hand-deriving a closed-form recursion (like Fig. 4's Sierpinski rule), represent the local operator as a small tensor/stencil, "roll" it (translate it) across the full lattice to build the complete linear map, and compute the null space of that map directly to get all valid \(\{t_p\}\) at once — then read the 3D (or nD) symmetries off the kernel basis.

**Assessment: this is sound and is a genuine generalization.**

### 7.1 What "rolling" is, precisely

Pick one qubit per lattice site (translation-invariant, single species for now). A local operator's support pattern relative to some origin — the set of offsets \((a,b,c)\) it touches — is encoded as a polynomial

$$f(x,y,z) = \sum_{(a,b,c)} x^a y^b z^c \pmod 2.$$

"Rolling" \(f\) across every site of an \(L_x\times L_y\times L_z\) periodic lattice and taking products with exponents \(t_p\) is literally evaluating the convolution

$$(f * t)(\vec r) = \sum_{\vec r'} f(\vec r - \vec r')\, t(\vec r') \pmod 2,$$

which is exactly multiplication by \(f(x,y,z)\) inside the finite group ring

$$R_L \;=\; \mathbb{F}_2[x,y,z]\big/(x^{L_x}-1,\; y^{L_y}-1,\; z^{L_z}-1).$$

Concretely, if you build the map as a matrix \(F\) (size \(N\times N\), \(N=L_xL_yL_z\)) whose rows are cyclic shifts of the flattened stencil of \(f\), then "rolling and multiplying" is just \(F\,\vec t \pmod 2\), and \(F\) is a **block-circulant (multi-level Toeplitz) matrix over \(\mathbb{F}_2\)**. Your tensor-with-periodic-shifts implementation *is* this matrix, built implicitly instead of explicitly.

### 7.2 Where \(N_c\) comes from

The condition \(\prod_p O_p^{t_p} = \mathbb{1}\) is, in this language, exactly

$$F\vec t \equiv 0 \pmod 2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec t \in \ker F.$$

So:

$$2N_c = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_2}\ker F = N - \mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2}(F).$$

This is nothing more than rank–nullity applied to the specific matrix \(F\) that your rolling procedure constructs — one call to a GF(2) null-space routine on \(F\) hands you \(N_c\) directly (as half the kernel dimension), plus an explicit basis for the \(2N_c\)-dimensional solution space, all at once, for whatever \(L_x, L_y, L_z\) you chose. This is why the approach is attractive: you never need to spot the Sierpinski/Pascal's-triangle pattern by eye — the kernel basis *is* the generalization of Fig. 4's recursion, computed automatically, for any local operator and any dimension.

**Worked mini-example (1D, single generator).** Take a 2-site operator \(f(x) = 1+x\) (nearest-neighbor product, e.g. a domain-wall-type check) on a ring of \(L\) sites. Then \(F\) is the circulant matrix for multiplication by \((1+x)\) in \(\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^L-1)\). Its kernel is the ideal of elements killed by \((1+x)\); since \((1+x)\) divides \(x^L-1\) whenever \(L\) is even (as \(x^L-1=(x-1)^L\) in char. 2, so \((1+x)\mid(x^L-1)\) for any \(L\ge1\)), the quotient ring is never a field and \(\ker F\) is generically 1-dimensional, giving \(N_c=1/2\)... — the point of walking through this by hand is exactly to show why you *want* the automated nullspace computation: the kernel dimension depends sensitively on how \(f(x)\) divides \(x^L-1\) mod 2, i.e. on number-theoretic properties of \(L\) (this is the "UV/IR mixing" you saw earlier). A direct GF(2) rank computation on \(F\) sidesteps having to work out this divisibility by hand for every \(L\).

### 7.3 Symmetry extraction and multi-species/scaling caveats (as before, now cross-referenced to §7.1–7.2)

- **Symmetry extraction (see §3):** the kernel basis vectors of \(F\) give the right *support pattern*, but the physical membrane/symmetry operator is the **truncated/open** version of that pattern (stop the convolution partway across the lattice instead of wrapping all the way around), not the full closed relation.
- **Multi-species subtlety:** with only one generator type, \(F\) is a single circulant block and the kernel computation is essentially a gcd-type question (as in the 1D example above), tractable even symbolically. With several inequivalent generator types on the same unit cell (e.g. Haah's cubic code, or CSS codes with separate X/Z checks), \(F\) becomes a **block matrix of circulants** — equivalently a matrix of polynomials — and \(\ker F\) is a *module (syzygy)* computation over \(\mathbb{F}_2[x,y,z]\), a ring that is *not* a PID in ≥2 variables. This is why Haah's closed-form cubic-code GSD formula needed extra number theory (resultants, roots of \(x^L-1\) in extension fields). Numerically, for any *fixed* \(L\), this is sidestepped entirely — \(F\) is just a large but finite binary matrix, and \(\ker F\) is computed by ordinary linear algebra — at the cost of not getting a closed-form \(L\)-dependence.
- **Scaling:** naive Gaussian elimination on \(F\) is \(O(N^3)=O((L_xL_yL_z)^3)\) and becomes intractable well before interesting 3D sizes (e.g. \(L=32\) in 3D gives \(N=32768\), already \(\sim10^{13}\) operations). Because \(F\) is block-circulant, the standard trick — and effectively what Haah's polynomial method does symbolically — is to factor \(x^{L_x}-1\), \(y^{L_y}-1\), \(z^{L_z}-1\) into irreducibles over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) and use the Chinese Remainder Theorem to block-diagonalize \(R_L\) into a direct sum of small quotient rings (a GF(2) analogue of a Fourier/eigen-decomposition of a circulant matrix). Each block gives its own small rank computation, and

$$2N_c = \sum_{\text{blocks } i} \dim\ker\big(F|_{\text{block }i}\big),$$

  turning one huge nullspace call into many cheap per-mode ones. Short of implementing the CRT decomposition yourself, dedicated bit-packed GF(2) linear-algebra libraries (M4RI/M4RIE, the `galois` Python package, Sage's \(\mathbb{F}_2\) matrix routines) will get substantially further than a generic dense solver before you need it.

**Bottom line:** the approach correctly generalizes the degeneracy-counting machinery to 3D/nD numerically — \(N_c\) is just half the kernel dimension of the block-circulant matrix your rolling operation builds. The two things to build in are (1) truncating kernel vectors to extract true membrane/symmetry operators rather than reading them off the closed relations, and (2) either accepting per-\(L\) numerics or adding CRT-based block-diagonalization once system sizes make brute-force nullspace too slow.
